What the policy is about

Google's misrepresentation policy covers two things. One is hiding or blurring who you are: no real business behind the store, no way to reach anyone, a name that doesn't match anywhere. The other is offers that mislead: prices that change at checkout, discounts against a price nobody ever paid, claims you can't back up, products that look like a brand they aren't.

The reviewer isn't checking a formal list item by item. They're answering a question: would a shopper who buys here know who they're dealing with and get what they paid for? Everything below feeds into that answer.

Identity: the most common cause

In most misrepresentation cases the store doesn't show a real business. Look at your site as a stranger would:

  • Is there a business name, and is it the same on the site, in Merchant Center Business info, in the checkout and on the payment receipt?
  • Is there a physical address? A PO box or "online only" with nothing else doesn't help.
  • Is there a phone number or at least a working email on your own domain? A Gmail address and a contact form alone look weak.
  • Does the About page say something specific: where the goods come from, who runs the shop, since when?

Dropshipping stores fail here most often, because the supplier's name appears in the packaging or tracking, the store's name appears on the site, and nothing connects the two.

Terms a shopper needs before paying

  • Returns and refunds on a separate page: number of days, condition of the item, who pays return shipping, how and when the money comes back.
  • Shipping: where you ship, cost, delivery time. If delivery takes three weeks because goods come from abroad, say so.
  • Payment: the methods you accept, shown before checkout.
  • Total price visible before payment. Fees or shipping that appear only at the last step are a misrepresentation trigger on their own.

Offers that look misleading

  • Permanent "−70%" where the crossed-out price was never real.
  • Countdown timers that restart when the page reloads, "only 3 left" counters that never change.
  • Reviews imported from a marketplace or written by the shop, especially with five stars across the board.
  • Health, weight-loss or earning claims you can't prove.
  • Product titles or images that suggest a brand you don't sell (a "style" of a famous item named after it).

Content that says "nobody is here"

Supplier descriptions copied across hundreds of products, theme demo text left in the footer, social icons linking to the theme author's profile, empty collections. Each of these alone may pass. Together they tell the reviewer that the store was set up quickly and nobody maintains it.

A working order of fixes

  1. Make the business identity consistent: one name, one address, one phone, the same in the footer, Contact page, checkout and Merchant Center.
  2. Write proper Returns, Shipping and Privacy pages and link them in the footer of every page.
  3. Go through checkout on a phone and check that the total, shipping and payment methods are shown before paying.
  4. Remove fake urgency, unprovable claims and inflated "was" prices. Keep only reviews you can trace to real orders.
  5. Rewrite descriptions at least for the products in the feed, starting with the bestsellers.
  6. Check that the feed matches the site: price, currency, availability.
  7. Only then request a review. One review, with everything already in place.

When the store looks fine and it's still rejected

Then the problem is often outside the store. Look at the domain history (did it host something else before?), at other accounts linked to the same business or payment profile, and at the Google Ads account linked to Merchant Center. A suspended Ads account or an unfinished advertiser verification can stand behind a Merchant Center decision. This is also the stage where a second pair of eyes helps most, because after three rounds you stop noticing what's on your own site.
